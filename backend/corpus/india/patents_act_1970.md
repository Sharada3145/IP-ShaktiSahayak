# Patents Act, 1970 (as amended)

The Patents Act, 1970 is the primary legislation governing patent law in India. It was significantly amended in 2005 to comply with India's TRIPS obligations and has been further amended with the 2024 Rules.

## Section 2 — Definitions

Section 2(1)(j) defines "invention" as a new product or process involving an inventive step and capable of industrial application. This definition is central to determining patentability of Ayurvedic innovations.

Section 2(1)(ja) defines "inventive step" as a feature of an invention that involves technical advance as compared to the existing knowledge or having economic significance or both that makes the invention not obvious to a person skilled in the art.

Section 2(1)(l) defines "new invention" as any invention or technology which has not been anticipated by publication in any document or used in the country or elsewhere in the world before the date of filing of patent application with complete specification.

## Section 3 — What Are Not Inventions

Section 3 lists categories of subject matter that are NOT considered inventions and therefore NOT patentable:

### Section 3(d) — New Forms of Known Substances

Section 3(d) provides that the mere discovery of a new form of a known substance which does not result in the enhancement of the known efficacy of that substance is NOT patentable. The mere discovery of any new property or new use for a known substance is also NOT patentable. For the purposes of this clause, salts, esters, ethers, polymorphs, metabolites, pure form, particle size, isomers, mixtures of isomers, complexes, combinations, and other derivatives of known substance shall be considered to be the same substance, unless they differ significantly in properties with regard to efficacy.

This section is highly relevant to Ayurvedic innovations. If a researcher discovers a new form of a known Ayurvedic compound (e.g., a nano-formulation of curcumin), they must demonstrate ENHANCED EFFICACY over the known substance to obtain a patent.

### Section 3(p) — Traditional Knowledge

Section 3(p) provides that an invention which, in effect, is traditional knowledge or which is an aggregation or duplication of known properties of traditionally known component or components is NOT patentable.

This is the most critical provision for Ayurveda. Classical formulations described in authoritative Ayurvedic texts (such as Charaka Samhita, Sushruta Samhita, Ashtanga Hridaya) are considered traditional knowledge and are barred from patenting under Section 3(p).

However, if a researcher makes a genuine inventive modification to a traditional formulation — such as a novel extraction process, a new combination with demonstrably synergistic effects not described in traditional texts, or a new delivery mechanism — the resulting innovation may be patentable provided it meets the requirements of novelty, inventive step, and industrial applicability.

### Section 3(e) — Substances from Admixture

Section 3(e) bars patentability of a substance obtained by a mere admixture resulting only in the aggregation of the properties of the components thereof, or a process for producing such substance. This is relevant to Ayurvedic polyherbal formulations — if the combination merely aggregates known properties without synergy, it is not patentable.

### Section 3(i) — Methods of Treatment

Section 3(i) provides that any process for the medicinal, surgical, curative, prophylactic, diagnostic, therapeutic or other treatment of human beings is NOT patentable. This means a method of Ayurvedic treatment (e.g., a specific Panchakarma protocol) cannot be patented, though the products used in such treatment may be.

## Section 25 — Opposition to Patents

Section 25(1) provides for pre-grant opposition and Section 25(2) provides for post-grant opposition. Any person can file a pre-grant opposition, while only a "person interested" can file a post-grant opposition.

Grounds for opposition include:
- The invention was publicly known or publicly used in India before the priority date
- The invention was anticipated by prior publication (including TKDL records)
- The invention is obvious and does not involve an inventive step
- The subject matter is not patentable under Section 3 (including 3(d) and 3(p))
- The complete specification does not sufficiently describe the invention

The TKDL (Traditional Knowledge Digital Library) has been extensively used in opposition proceedings to prevent patents on traditional knowledge-based inventions at patent offices worldwide.

## Section 48 — Rights of Patentees

Section 48 grants the patentee the exclusive right to prevent third parties from making, using, offering for sale, selling, or importing the patented product or process in India.

## Section 64 — Revocation of Patents

Section 64 provides grounds for revocation of a granted patent, including many of the same grounds as opposition. Patents on traditional knowledge that were granted erroneously can be challenged through revocation proceedings.

## Section 107A — Bolar Provision

Section 107A permits the making, constructing, using, selling, or importing a patented invention solely for uses reasonably related to obtaining regulatory approval. This is relevant for generic Ayurvedic drug development.

## Patent Rules, 2024

The Patent Rules, 2024 introduce updated procedures for patent filing, examination, and opposition. Key changes include:
- Expedited examination provisions
- Updated fee structures
- Digital filing requirements
- Modified timelines for examination and response
- Updated requirements for biological material deposits
